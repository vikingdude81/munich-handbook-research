When the Egyptian named his temple the house of the god,
the name was a literal expression of his belief: the deity dwelt
in the temple, as a man lives in his house, and the priests,
the servafits of the god, who supplied him with food and attend-
ance, were his household servants. This appears also in the
religious ceremonies, and perhaps in the arrangement of the
temple buildings-although in historical times this belief can
have been little more than an obsolete idea.
Each temple must originally have been dedicated to one
single deity who was considered to be its loyd, but owing to the
natural anxiety to secure the favour of other gods for the city
others were added as secondary deities, and in the greater

ries. Two of these, a goddess and a god, were generally regarded

At the present day we are accustomed to see the magnificent

them was shut out by the high wall that enclosed the precincts,
and rendered them pure and peaceful spots in the midst of an

through the streets of the town, but it was necessary to make a
